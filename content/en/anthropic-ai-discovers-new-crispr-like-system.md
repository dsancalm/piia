Anthropic has built a life sciences research group and an in-house lab in the Bay Area. The team uses Claude to scan massive DNA databases, generate hypotheses, and test them experimentally. Claude autonomously discovered a new enzymatic system linked to a DNA repeat array that resembles CRISPR. The system relies on a reverse transcriptase (RT) found in a jumbo bacteriophage. The RT was already known, but Claude identified the non-coding repeat array and an accessory protein of unknown function for the first time. The system is called ART, for Array-Associated Reverse Transcriptases, and it is found mainly in bacteriophages.

ART has three parts: the RT, an adjacent companion gene, and a long array of evenly spaced DNA repeats. Feng Zhang at MIT and the Broad Institute called the finding exciting and worthy of further work. The computational search took 21 hours, involved about 950 Claude agents running in parallel, and consumed 210 million tokens. The agents collected more than 200,000 RTs, proposed 3,500 candidate systems, and narrowed the list to 20 for detailed analysis. Human scientists performed the lab work, including protein expression, biochemical characterization, and structural studies. The lab operates at BSL-1 and BSL-2 levels with no human pathogens. A preprint describing the discovery is available.

What is not known: the primary biological function of ART, the exact molecular mechanism of the RT, the accessory protein, and the repeat array, whether ART is programmable for gene editing or other biotechnological uses, the three-dimensional structure of its components, the results of ongoing biochemical and structural assays, and the date and journal for the final publication of the preprint.
